Do GLP-1 Drugs Help Obstructive Sleep Apnea?

October 11, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes, for adults who have both obesity and moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). One drug in this family has an FDA indication for it: Zepbound (tirzepatide), approved on December 20, 2024. In its two 52-week trials, breathing events fell by 25 to 29 per hour on Zepbound, compared with about 5 per hour on placebo. The benefit tracks weight loss, and the trials did not test whether people can stop CPAP.

Which GLP-1 drugs are FDA-approved for sleep apnea?

Only one. The Zepbound label says the drug is indicated, with a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity, "to treat moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) in adults with obesity." The FDA announcement called it "the first drug treatment option for certain patients with obstructive sleep apnea."

Three details in that wording matter:

  • Moderate to severe. The label's trials required an apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) of 15 or more. AHI is the number of breathing pauses and shallow breaths per hour of sleep. Mild OSA is outside the indication.
  • Adults with obesity. The trials required a body mass index (BMI) of 30 or higher. The indication does not cover people with OSA who do not have obesity.
  • Tirzepatide is a dual drug. The label describes it as a GIP receptor and GLP-1 receptor agonist. It is usually grouped with GLP-1 drugs, but it acts on two hormone receptors.

Other drugs in the class do not carry the indication. The current Wegovy (semaglutide) label and Saxenda (liraglutide) label do not mention sleep apnea. Mounjaro contains the same tirzepatide as Zepbound, but its label covers type 2 diabetes and does not list OSA. Our sister site mounjaro.md covers that brand. Our GLP-1 medications comparison covers the whole class.

What did the Zepbound sleep apnea trials show?

The approval rests on SURMOUNT-OSA, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2024. It was two randomized, placebo-controlled trials with 469 adults in total. All had moderate to severe OSA and obesity, and none had type 2 diabetes. Everyone received diet and activity counseling. Treatment lasted 52 weeks.

Mean AHI at baseline was 51.5 events per hour in the first trial and 49.5 in the second. The label counts 30 or more as severe. Mean BMI was about 39.

The label reports these results at week 52:

  • Trial without PAP (234 adults who could not or would not use positive airway pressure): AHI fell by 25.3 events per hour on Zepbound and 5.3 on placebo. That is a 50.7% drop compared with 3%.
  • Trial with PAP (235 adults already using it): AHI fell by 29.3 events per hour on Zepbound and 5.5 on placebo. That is a 58.7% drop compared with 2.5%.
  • AHI cut by half or more: 61.2% and 72.4% of people on Zepbound, compared with 19% and 23.3% on placebo.
  • Remission or mild OSA without sleepiness: 42.2% and 50.2% on Zepbound, compared with 15.9% and 14.3% on placebo. The label defines this as an AHI under 5, or an AHI of 5 to 14 with a normal sleepiness score.
  • Body weight: down 17.7% and 19.6% on Zepbound, compared with 1.6% and 2.3% on placebo.

Read the averages with care. A mean drop of 25 to 29 events, from a mean baseline near 50, leaves many people with sleep apnea at week 52.

Is the benefit just from weight loss?

Mostly, as far as anyone can show. The FDA announcement attributes the OSA improvement to the reduction in body weight. A 2025 follow-up analysis of SURMOUNT-OSA found that the size of the AHI change was associated with the amount of weight lost in both trials.

That analysis also looked at timing with a home sleep device in the first trial. The difference from placebo did not become statistically significant until week 20. The benefit builds slowly.

A 2026 review in JAMA Otolaryngology reaches the same view. It calls weight loss "the primary established mechanism," and says that weight-independent effects are supported only by early laboratory evidence. It concludes these drugs are unlikely to help people without obesity.

Do semaglutide or liraglutide help sleep apnea?

Liraglutide has one sizeable randomized trial. The SCALE Sleep Apnea trial assigned 359 adults with obesity and moderate or severe OSA to liraglutide 3.0 mg or placebo for 32 weeks. None were using CPAP. AHI fell by 12.2 events per hour on liraglutide and 6.1 on placebo, a difference of 6.1. Weight fell 5.7% compared with 1.6%. Both the AHI change and the weight loss were smaller than in the tirzepatide trials. The drugs were never compared head to head.

Semaglutide has weaker evidence for OSA itself. Our PubMed search found no published randomized trial of semaglutide that used AHI as its main outcome. A 2026 study of health records matched 191,273 semaglutide users with obesity to the same number of non-users. A new OSA diagnosis was less likely in the semaglutide group (hazard ratio 0.62). A record study can show a link. It cannot prove cause.

Can you stop CPAP if you take a GLP-1 drug?

Not on your own, and the trials do not answer the question. The Zepbound label says: "The clinical studies for OSA did not evaluate the timing or appropriateness of PAP discontinuation in patients who were previously compliant with PAP therapy." In the trial of PAP users, people paused PAP for 7 days before the week-52 sleep study.

The FDA announcement describes PAP as "the standard of care for moderate to severe OSA." The JAMA review estimates that GLP-1 drugs lower AHI by about 22 events per hour, compared with about 31 for CPAP. It places the drugs as a partner to CPAP, and notes that only 30% to 60% of people keep using CPAP.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's note on the approval does not tell patients to stop PAP. If your weight has dropped a lot, ask your sleep clinician about a repeat sleep study. That test shows whether your treatment should change. If you have not been tested yet, insomnia.md lists the common signs of sleep apnea.

What happens to sleep apnea if you stop the drug?

No trial we found has measured AHI after people stop tirzepatide. SURMOUNT-OSA ended at 52 weeks with everyone still on treatment.

What is known is the weight pattern. In the SURMOUNT-4 trial, adults took tirzepatide for 36 weeks and lost 20.9% of their weight on average. Half then switched to placebo. Over the next 52 weeks, weight rose 14.0% in the placebo group and fell a further 5.5% in the group that stayed on the drug.

Because the OSA benefit tracks weight, regained weight would be expected to bring breathing events back. That is an inference, not a measured result. Our guide on what happens when you stop GLP-1 drugs has more detail.

What does the label say about dose and safety?

The Zepbound label sets a starting dosage of 2.5 mg once weekly, raised in 2.5 mg steps at least 4 weeks apart. For OSA, the recommended maintenance dosage is 10 mg or 15 mg once weekly.

Side effects in the OSA trials matched the weight-loss trials. The NEJM report says the most frequent ones were gastrointestinal and mostly mild to moderate. The FDA announcement lists warnings that include pancreatitis and gallbladder problems. Our GLP-1 side effects guide covers these.

Two label points matter to people with OSA. The label warns of rare reports of pulmonary aspiration during general anesthesia or deep sedation, so tell your surgical team you take the drug. It also says Zepbound should not be combined with other tirzepatide products or with any GLP-1 receptor agonist.

The bottom line

GLP-1 drugs can improve obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity, and the benefit follows weight loss. Zepbound is the only one with an FDA indication, for moderate to severe OSA in adults with obesity. In its trials, AHI fell by 25 to 29 events per hour over 52 weeks, and 42% to 50% of people reached remission or mild disease. The trials did not test stopping CPAP or stopping the drug.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Zepbound approved for mild sleep apnea?

No. The label indication is for moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity. The trials behind it enrolled people with an apnea-hypopnea index of 15 or more and a BMI of 30 or more.

Is Wegovy or Ozempic approved for sleep apnea?

The current Wegovy label does not list sleep apnea as an indication. Semaglutide has health-record studies that link it to fewer new sleep apnea diagnoses, but we found no published randomized trial with breathing events as the main outcome. Zepbound is the only drug in this group with an FDA indication for obstructive sleep apnea.

How long does Zepbound take to improve sleep apnea?

The main trials measured the result at 52 weeks. A follow-up analysis that used a home sleep device found the difference from placebo was not statistically significant until week 20. The improvement builds as weight comes down.

Does Zepbound cure sleep apnea?

The trials do not show a cure. At week 52, 42.2% to 50.2% of people on Zepbound had remission or mild sleep apnea without sleepiness, compared with 14.3% to 15.9% on placebo. The rest still had more than that, and no trial has tracked breathing after the drug is stopped.

Can someone with sleep apnea who is not overweight take a GLP-1 drug for it?

The FDA indication covers adults with obesity only, and the trials did not enroll people with a BMI under 30. A 2026 review in JAMA Otolaryngology says these drugs are unlikely to benefit people without obesity or people whose airway blockage is mainly anatomical.
